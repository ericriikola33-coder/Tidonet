Pay yourself first, and what to do with a raise

5 min read · Tidonet Guides

Most people plan to save whatever is left at the end of the month. The trouble is that there is rarely anything left. Money that sits in an everyday account tends to find something to do.

"Pay yourself first" turns the order around. On payday, a fixed amount moves to savings before anything else happens. You then live on the rest. It is the same money, but the decision is made once instead of every day.

Why the order matters

Willpower is a poor savings plan. An automatic transfer does not get tired, does not have a bad week and does not see a sale. People who save first usually do not feel poorer after a month or two, because spending quietly adjusts to what is in the account.

Lifestyle creep

Lifestyle creep is what happens when every pay rise turns into higher spending. A slightly nicer phone plan, more takeaway, a bigger subscription. None of it feels like a decision, and a year later the raise is gone and saving is as hard as before.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying a raise. The problem is only when all of it disappears without you choosing where it went.

The half-and-half rule

A simple way to handle a raise: spend half, keep half. Half goes to things you enjoy, with no guilt. The other half goes straight to savings or investments, before you get used to having it.

Take a raise of 500 a month after tax. Half for fun is 250 a month. The other 250, invested at an average of 7% a year, could grow like this:

30 years90,000≈ 305,000

And you still had an extra 250 a month to enjoy the whole time.

The key idea: you never miss money you never got used to spending. The best moment to start saving part of a raise is the first month you receive it.

How to set it up

  • Pick a date. Schedule the transfer for payday or the day after.
  • Start small if you need to. A small automatic amount beats a large amount you keep postponing.
  • Keep it out of sight. A separate account makes it less tempting to dip into.
  • Raise it when your pay rises. Half of every raise is an easy rule to remember.

Larry and Bella got the same raise

In our video, Larry spends all of his raise and ends the year with zero savings and a gold nightcap. "Worth it," he says. Bella's answer is the whole idea in one line: half for fun, half for future me.

A fair warning

The table uses a steady 7% average return to show the pattern. Real returns vary from year to year and are never guaranteed. If you have expensive debt, paying that down first is often worth more than investing.
