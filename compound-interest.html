Compound interest, explained simply

4 min read · Tidonet Guides

Compound interest is what happens when your money earns money, and then that new money starts earning money too. It sounds small. Over many years it becomes the biggest force in personal finance.

A snowball rolling downhill

Picture a small snowball at the top of a hill. As it rolls, it picks up more snow. The bigger it gets, the more snow it picks up with every turn. Money works the same way. In the first years, growth feels slow. Later, the growth on top of growth takes over.

A simple example

Say you invest 100 a month and earn 7% a year on average. Here is roughly what you would have:

AfterYou put inTotal value
10 years12,000≈ 17,300
20 years24,000≈ 52,100
30 years36,000≈ 122,000

Look at the jump between year 20 and year 30. You only added 12,000 more, but the total grew by about 70,000. That is compounding doing the heavy lifting.

The key idea: time matters more than the amount. Someone who starts with a small amount early often ends up ahead of someone who starts with a bigger amount later.

Three things that make it work

  • Time. The longer your money stays invested, the stronger the effect.
  • Consistency. Adding money every month keeps feeding the snowball.
  • Leaving it alone. Taking money out early stops the growth on top of growth.

A fair warning

Real investments do not grow by the same percentage every year. Some years go up, some go down. A yearly average is a helpful way to think about the long term, but it is never guaranteed. Fees also matter, because they compound against you in the same way returns compound for you.
