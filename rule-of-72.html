The rule of 72

4 min read · Tidonet Guides

The rule of 72 is a shortcut you can do in your head. Divide 72 by a yearly growth rate, and you get roughly how many years it takes for an amount to double.

Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ yearly rate

At 6% a year, money doubles in about 12 years, because 72 ÷ 6 = 12. At 8%, it takes about 9 years.

How close is it?

Surprisingly close. Here is the shortcut next to the exact answer:

Yearly rateRule of 72Exact
2%36 years35.0 years
3%24 years23.4 years
4%18 years17.7 years
6%12 years11.9 years
7%10 years10.2 years
8%9 years9.0 years
10%7 years7.3 years
12%6 years6.1 years

For the rates most people meet in everyday life, the shortcut lands within about a year of the exact answer, which is close enough for thinking and comparing.

Doubling adds up fast

The interesting part is what happens after the first doubling. Say 1,000 is invested at an average of 7% a year. It doubles about every ten years:

  • After about 10 years: 2,000
  • After about 20 years: 4,000
  • After about 30 years: 8,000
  • After about 40 years: 16,000

Each step adds more than all the steps before it combined. That is why people who start early often end up far ahead, even if they invest less in total. Ten extra years is not ten extra years of growth. It is one whole extra doubling.

The rule works against you too

The same shortcut applies to anything that grows by a percentage.

  • Debt. A credit card at 24% doubles what you owe in about 3 years if nothing is paid (72 ÷ 24 = 3).
  • Prices. With inflation at 3% a year, prices double in about 24 years. Money kept as cash buys half as much by then.
  • Fees. A 2% yearly fee does not sound like much, but it slows every doubling of your savings.

How to use it

Use it to compare, not to predict. When you see two rates, divide 72 by each and compare the doubling times. The difference between 4% and 8% is not "four percent". It is the difference between doubling every 18 years and every 9.

A fair warning

The rule assumes the same rate every year. Real investments go up and down, and no return is guaranteed. Treat the answer as a rough picture of the long term, not a promise.
