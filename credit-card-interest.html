How credit card interest works

5 min read · Tidonet Guides

A credit card is a short loan that renews itself every month. If you pay the whole bill on time, the loan is usually free. If you do not, the card starts charging interest, and that interest is among the most expensive kinds of borrowing most people ever use.

What APR means

APR stands for annual percentage rate. It is the yearly price of borrowing. A card with a 24% APR charges about 2% of your unpaid balance every month. That sounds small, but it is charged on whatever you still owe, including interest from earlier months. This is compound interest working against you instead of for you.

The 1,000 example

Say you buy something for 1,000 on a card with a 24% APR and stop using the card. How long it takes to clear depends almost entirely on how much you pay each month:

You payTime to clearTotal paidInterest
25 a month6 years 10 months≈ 2,030≈ 1,030
50 a month2 years 2 months≈ 1,290≈ 290
100 a month1 year≈ 1,130≈ 130
200 a month6 months≈ 1,060≈ 60

At 25 a month, the purchase takes almost seven years to pay off and costs about twice the price on the tag. Doubling the payment to 50 cuts the time to a little over two years. The balance is the same in every row. Only the payment changed.

The key idea: in the first months, most of a small payment goes to interest. Out of the first 25, about 20 is interest and only 5 reduces what you owe.

Why the minimum payment is a trap

Card companies let you pay a small minimum each month. It keeps your account in good standing, but it is designed to be small. Paying only the minimum means the balance shrinks very slowly while interest keeps being added. Many statements include a line that shows how long the minimum would take. It is worth reading once.

Four habits that keep cards cheap

  • Pay the full balance when you can. Most cards charge no interest on purchases if the whole bill is paid by the due date.
  • If you cannot, pay as much above the minimum as possible. The table shows how much each extra amount helps.
  • Pay on time, every time. Late fees and penalty rates make an expensive loan even more expensive. Automatic payments help.
  • Stop adding to a balance you are paying down. New purchases push the finish line further away.

Larry's TV

In one of our short videos, Larry buys a 1,000 TV on a 24% card and says "relax, it's only 25 a month". The TV breaks in year five. The payments end in year seven. Bella saves for the same TV first and pays half as much. The example is a joke, but the math in the table above is real.

A fair note

Rates, fees and minimum payment rules differ between cards and countries, and real cards calculate interest daily, so your own numbers will differ a little. The pattern does not change: the smaller the payment, the more the purchase costs.
